73: CHAPTER I
74: INTRODUCTION
75: Safe driving depends not only on road conditions, traffic regulations, and vehicle performance but also on the driver’s ability to remain alert while operating a vehicle. Driver drowsiness is a serious road-safety concern because reduced alertness can impair attention, reaction time, judgment, and decision-making while driving. In the Philippine setting, the Land Transportation Office, as reported by the Philippine Information Agency, emphasized that drivers should pull over and rest when they feel tired. Similarly, Fonseca and Ferreira identified driver drowsiness as a significant road-safety issue associated with impaired attentional control, slower reaction time, reduced decision-making ability, and an increased risk of crashes and severe injuries (Philippine Information Agency, 2025; Fonseca & Ferreira, 2025). When drivers continue operating a vehicle despite visible signs of drowsiness, their ability to maintain the sustained attention required for safe driving may become compromised.
76: In the Philippines, road traffic crashes remain a major public-safety issue. The World Health Organization Philippines and the Department of Transportation reported that road traffic deaths in the country increased by 39%, from 7,938 deaths in 2011 to 11,096 deaths in 2021. The same source reported that road traffic injuries are the leading cause of death among Filipinos aged 15 to 29 years old and cost approximately 2.6% of the country’s gross domestic product. In response to this concern, the Philippine Road Safety Action Plan 2023–2028 was launched as the country’s blueprint for road-safety initiatives, with the goal of reducing road traffic deaths by 35% by 2028 (World Health Organization Philippines & Department of Transportation, 2023). The Philippine Statistics Authority also reported that deaths due to land transport accidents reached 13,125 in 2023, accounting for 1.9% of all deaths in the country. Meanwhile, the Asian Transport Observatory reported that road crashes and serious injuries cost the Philippines approximately USD 11 billion in 2021, equivalent to about 3% of the country’s gross domestic product (Philippine Statistics Authority, 2025; Asian Transport Observatory, 2025). Although Philippine statistics do not separately identify the number of crashes associated with driver drowsiness, Reyes (2025) reported that international estimates attribute up to 30% of road accidents to driver fatigue. The same report explained that sleepiness associated with drowsy driving slows reaction time, decreases awareness, impairs judgment, and increases the risk of crashes. These conditions demonstrate the need for preventive and technology-supported road-safety systems that can assist drivers before dangerous situations become more serious.
77: Human behavior and driver condition are also important considerations in road safety. Segun et al. (2024) identified human behavior, public awareness, infrastructure, technological interventions, and enforcement as factors associated with road-crash incidents in Metro Manila. Road safety therefore involves not only road and vehicle conditions but also the driver’s physical and mental condition. Because drowsiness may reduce alertness and responsiveness, technologies that monitor visible signs of drowsiness may help drivers recognize possible declines in attentiveness while driving.
78: Emerging technologies in intelligent transportation provide new methods for detecting possible driver drowsiness before it develops into a more serious safety risk. Computer vision and artificial intelligence have been widely explored in driver-drowsiness detection systems because they can analyze visible indicators such as eye closure, yawning, mouth movement, and changes in head posture. Albadawi et al. (2022) explained that advances in computing technology and artificial intelligence have contributed to the development of more capable driver-drowsiness detection systems. However, the practical implementation of these systems may still be affected by lighting conditions, device capability, detection reliability, and system accessibility. These limitations highlight the need for a driver-monitoring system designed to operate under practical in-vehicle conditions.
79: The background of this study is grounded in the integration of computer vision, artificial intelligence, the Internet of Things, and mobile-based driver monitoring. Camera-based systems can observe selected visible signs of drowsiness without requiring wearable sensors or direct vehicle integration. Lighting conditions remain an important consideration because camera-based facial measurements depend on usable image quality. DriveAlert uses an infrared-capable camera, and its performance under different available-light conditions will be evaluated using the final hardware configuration. Local processing is also important because warning systems should respond promptly even when internet access or mobile data is unavailable. Edge and localized processing can reduce latency and support privacy-sensitive Internet of Things applications, while ESP32 documentation supports Wi-Fi capability and local communication between the IoT device and the smartphone (Andriulo et al., 2024; Espressif Systems, 2025; Espressif Systems, 2026). When combined with Android-based processing and local offline Wi-Fi communication, these technologies can support a more accessible driver-drowsiness monitoring system.
80: This study proposes DriveAlert, an Internet of Things-based driver drowsiness monitoring and alert system that uses computer vision and artificial intelligence. The system is designed to monitor selected visible signs of drowsiness, specifically prolonged eye closure, yawning, and head nodding. When a selected behavior satisfies its established duration, repetition, or temporal-confirmation condition, DriveAlert activates a warning alert. Threshold-based alerting is appropriate because driver-drowsiness detection systems should not rely on a single uncertain video frame and should instead evaluate whether a visible behavior is sustained or repeated before activating a warning (Albadawi et al., 2023; Essel et al., 2025). DriveAlert also stores distinct confirmed warning events for Driver review and applies staged intervention when qualifying warning activity recurs or persists. Warning Stage 1 provides the initial selected warning sound, and Warning Stage 2 retains that sound as an intermediate warning state. Warning Stage 3 combines the selected sound with a prerecorded spoken rest advisory and introduces Trusted Contact synchronization eligibility. These Warning Stages represent levels of system intervention and do not represent medical or physiological drowsiness severity.
81: Rationale of the Study
82: Driver drowsiness may not always be recognized immediately while a person is operating a vehicle. Although drivers may stop and rest once they become aware that they are sleepy, observable changes associated with reduced alertness may appear before they respond to their condition. Rajkumarsingh and Totah (2021) explained that drowsiness can affect judgment and decision-making, while Ayas et al. (2024) found that driver-state monitoring systems commonly combine detection with warning interventions. This creates a need for a supplementary system that can monitor selected visible signs of drowsiness and provide a timely warning without replacing the driver’s responsibility to stop and rest.
83: Visible changes involving the eyes, mouth, and head are commonly examined in driver-drowsiness monitoring because they can be observed without attaching physiological sensors to the driver. Rajkumarsingh and Totah (2021) considered blinking duration, yawning frequency, and head position, while Albadawi et al. (2023) examined eye condition, mouth opening, and head pose as visual features associated with drowsiness. These findings are relevant to the selected indicators monitored by DriveAlert: prolonged eye closure, yawning, and head nodding. Monitoring these behaviors provides a non-contact method of identifying observable changes that may be associated with reduced alertness.
84: However, ordinary driver behavior may resemble a visible sign of drowsiness. Brief eye closure may represent normal blinking, mouth opening may occur while speaking, and downward head movement may result from briefly looking at another area inside the vehicle. Responding to every isolated movement may therefore produce unnecessary warnings. Albadawi et al. (2023) evaluated visual indicators across a temporal window, while Quiles-Cucarella et al. (2024) and Essel et al. (2025) applied time-based or consecutive-frame conditions before activating warnings. These approaches demonstrate the importance of confirming that a behavior is sustained or repeated rather than relying on a single uncertain observation.
85: Warning reliability is also important because detection has limited value when the resulting alert is unclear, easily ignored, or repeatedly activated by incorrect detections. Ayas et al. (2024) found that driver-drowsiness mitigation systems commonly use auditory warnings and may apply different levels of intervention. Weibull et al. (2024) also showed that false alarms can influence how drivers respond to later warnings. A drowsiness-warning system must therefore balance responsiveness and consistency so that the driver is alerted when the established detection conditions are satisfied without producing excessive interruptions during ordinary movements.
86: DriveAlert applies three Warning Stages to manage recurrent or persistent qualifying warning activity. The first distinct confirmed warning event occurring while no Warning Stage is active immediately activates Stage 1 and begins a 60-second evaluation period. If another distinct confirmed warning event occurs while Stage 1 is active, the system immediately progresses to Stage 2 and begins a new 60-second evaluation period. A subsequent distinct confirmed warning event during Stage 2 immediately activates Stage 3. When no new distinct event occurs, the system evaluates whether a qualifying behavior remains persistently active at the 60-second boundary. Persistent qualifying activity causes progression from Stage 1 to Stage 2 or from Stage 2 to Stage 3. If no qualifying behavior remains active, Stage 1 returns to the normal state, while Stage 2 de-escalates to Stage 1. Stage 3 is the maximum intervention level. Recurrent events during Stage 3 may reactivate its warning without restarting the current evaluation period. At the Stage 3 boundary, recurrent or persistent qualifying activity maintains Stage 3; otherwise, the system de-escalates to Stage 2. Stages 1 and 2 use the selected warning sound, while Stage 3 adds a spoken rest advisory and Trusted Contact synchronization eligibility. These stages describe system intervention rather than medical or physiological drowsiness severity.
87: An immediate warning addresses a detected condition at a particular moment, but the warning alone provides little information after it has ended. Without an organized record, the driver may be unable to review when an alert occurred, which visible sign was detected, or whether alerts appeared repeatedly during a monitoring session. Hidayat and Ibnugraha (2025) developed a system described as a driver-fatigue monitoring system that displayed detection results through a web-based dashboard, while Kashyap et al. (2025) stored driver-status information, detection timestamps, and historical records for later review. These studies show that recorded events can remain useful beyond the immediate warning by providing structured information for later review.
88: Stored drowsiness-alert events may also be summarized to make the recorded information easier to interpret. Retained alerts may be organized into alert counts, detected-sign summaries, the highest Warning Stage associated with their stored warning events, alert trends, and Stage 3 synchronization status. Monitoring-session summaries separately identify the highest intervention stage reached during each session. These summaries allow users to review patterns in the available records without examining every alert individually. Their purpose is descriptive and should not be interpreted as medical analysis, accident prediction, confirmation of drowsiness, or proof that a driver is safe or unsafe to continue driving.
89: Drowsiness-alert records may also support secondary awareness through an approved Trusted Contact. DriveAlert stores all distinct confirmed warning events for Driver review but limits external synchronization according to its Warning Stage 3 rule. When DriveAlert first enters Warning Stage 3, one Stage 3 transition record becomes eligible for Trusted Contact synchronization. Individual confirmed warning events occurring during the active Stage 3 period remain in the Driver’s alert history and do not each independently create synchronization records. When a 60-second Stage 3 evaluation period ends, DriveAlert creates one synchronization-eligible persistence update if at least one additional distinct confirmed warning event occurred during that period or a qualifying behavior remains persistently active at its boundary. Either condition maintains Stage 3 and begins another 60-second evaluation period. If neither condition is present, the system de-escalates to Stage 2 without creating a persistence update for the completed period.
90: The reviewed approaches provide visible-sign monitoring, warning delivery, event storage, dashboard viewing, physical alarm output, offline operation, and authorized record access in different combinations. Some systems concentrate mainly on detecting a visible sign and producing an immediate warning, while others emphasize historical records, dashboards, or remote monitoring. The need addressed by this study is therefore not the absence of driver-drowsiness technology but the opportunity to combine selected functions that support the driver during monitoring and preserve relevant information after a warning has been issued.
91: DriveAlert addresses these connected needs by monitoring prolonged eye closure, yawning, and head nodding and providing an audio warning when a selected behavior satisfies its established detection condition. It also stores distinct confirmed alert episodes locally, presents descriptive summaries of retained records for Driver review, and synchronizes only eligible Stage 3 transition records and Stage 3 persistence updates for access by an approved Trusted Contact when internet connectivity becomes available. Through this integration, DriveAlert connects visible-sign monitoring, warning delivery, event recording, record review, and authorized information sharing within one driver-focused system. It is intended as a supplementary driver-support tool and does not medically or physiologically confirm drowsiness, determine whether a driver is fit to continue driving, replace adequate rest, or control the vehicle.

94: Objectives of the Study
95: This study aims to develop DriveAlert, an Internet of Things-based system designed to assist drivers by monitoring selected visible signs of drowsiness, providing warning alerts, storing drowsiness-alert records, and synchronizing these records with a connected Trusted Contact when internet connectivity becomes available.
96: Specifically, this study pursues the following objectives:
97: To gather data on the occurrence of drowsiness and observable signs associated with it among drivers while driving.
98: To design and implement a computer-vision algorithm capable of detecting prolonged eye closure, yawning, and head nodding using driver-specific detection thresholds and temporal-confirmation conditions for triggering warning alerts.
99: To design and implement a local Wi-Fi communication architecture between the IoT device and the driver’s smartphone that enables real-time drowsiness-sign monitoring without requiring internet or mobile data connectivity.
100: To implement an alarm system in which the IoT device receives wireless warning commands from the smartphone and produces predefined audio warning alerts through an onboard speaker.
101: To develop a dashboard that records and displays drowsiness-alert events and synchronizes drowsiness-alert records with a connected Trusted Contact when internet connectivity becomes available.
102: Scope and Limitations
103: This study focuses on the development and pilot evaluation of DriveAlert, an IoT-based driver drowsiness monitoring and alert system using computer vision. The system monitors prolonged eye closure, yawning, and head nodding through an IoT device equipped with an infrared-capable camera and an onboard speaker. Dedicated infrared LEDs and external infrared illuminators are excluded from the final prototype.
104: An Android mobile application processes the captured video locally using calibrated EAR and MAR thresholds, a fixed 15° downward head-pitch deviation relative to the Driver’s calibrated neutral position, and sign-specific temporal-confirmation conditions. After a selected visible sign satisfies its complete confirmation condition, DriveAlert creates a confirmed warning event, applies the appropriate Warning Stage, requests the corresponding warning output, and stores the event locally for Driver review. Separate Stage 3 transition records and qualifying Stage 3 persistence updates become eligible for synchronization with an approved Trusted Contact when internet connectivity becomes available.
105: DriveAlert is intended for drivers of four-wheeled and larger vehicles. Motorcycles and other two-wheeled vehicles are excluded because of differences in driver position, camera placement, weather exposure, and operating conditions. The system will be evaluated only under controlled or simulated driving conditions and will not be tested for long-term use during actual road travel, heavy traffic, severe weather, uneven roads, or other unpredictable conditions.
106: DriveAlert is limited to monitoring three selected visible signs associated with drowsiness: prolonged eye closure, yawning, and head nodding. The system does not medically or physiologically confirm that a driver is drowsy. These behaviors may occur for reasons unrelated to drowsiness, while a drowsy driver may not always display them clearly. The system may therefore generate false alerts or miss relevant events. It does not detect sleep disorders, stress, illness, medication effects, alcohol influence, or other causes of reduced alertness, and it does not use physiological or wearable sensors.
107: Detection performance may be affected by camera placement, Driver position, head orientation, facial obstruction, eyeglasses, sunglasses, masks, glare, lighting conditions, sudden movement, reflections, and image quality. Although dedicated infrared illumination was included in the initial design, it was excluded from the final prototype due to limitations encountered during hardware development. The available infrared LED configuration did not provide sufficient facial illumination at the intended camera-to-Driver distance. Attempts to improve illumination by increasing the number of infrared LEDs also resulted in unstable operation of the breadboard power supply setup, including intermittent power loss and interruptions affecting connected components. These issues prevented the expanded LED configuration from operating reliably and limited further improvements to infrared illumination using the available hardware. These limitations apply specifically to the configuration tested in this study and do not establish that infrared illumination is unsuitable for driver monitoring in general. The final prototype therefore depends on available light, and its infrared-capable camera does not guarantee reliable assessment under extremely low-light or no-light conditions. DriveAlert pauses drowsiness-sign assessment when facial lighting is insufficient, even if a face remains detected. Assessment also pauses when reliable facial tracking is unavailable or fewer than two monitored signs remain available. Frames rejected because of insufficient lighting do not advance calibration’s required valid measurement time. Assessment resumes when the applicable visibility, lighting-quality, and observation-freshness requirements are restored. These assessment pauses do not by themselves end the monitoring session. DriveAlert also provides operational guidance that is separate from drowsiness-stage warnings. During eligible active monitoring, sustained inactivity may produce a “No movement detected” status after ten seconds and a spoken movement advisory after twenty-five seconds. These timings are provisional engineering settings. The advisory does not create a drowsiness-alert record, advance the Warning Stage, verify Driver identity, or establish that the camera input is from a live person.
108: DriveAlert uses guided facial-measurement calibration and spatial Driver-area selection rather than facial recognition or automatic Driver identification. Calibration records neutral facial measurements, closed-eye and open-mouth references, and the direction of downward head rotation. The face-processing pipeline may detect multiple face candidates, but drowsiness-sign assessment uses at most one selected Driver face. Selection depends on the configured Driver area and spatial continuity. Ambiguous, competing, or unreliable detections pause assessment. These safeguards do not verify identity or guarantee the exclusion of passengers in every scene. Camera movement, incorrect positioning, other visible faces, or changes in the Driver’s position may affect tracking and detection. When reliable facial tracking is unavailable, assessment pauses and the Driver is prompted to restore the camera view.
109: The system does not automatically identify individual drivers. In shared vehicles, each driver must use the correct DriveAlert account, connect to the IoT device, and verify camera alignment before monitoring begins. Otherwise, drowsiness-alert records may be stored under the wrong account. Facial recognition and automatic driver identification are outside the scope of the study.
110: System performance may depend on the Android smartphone’s processing power, memory, battery condition, and temperature. Devices with limited capability may experience slower video processing, delayed warnings, or reduced application stability. The pilot evaluation will use selected compatible Android devices; therefore, the results may not represent all Android devices and hardware configurations.
111: All distinct confirmed alert episodes are initially stored locally in the Driver’s alert history, subject to user deletion and configured retention. Descriptive alert summaries reflect retained records. Removing a shared record from a Trusted Contact’s personal history view does not delete the source Driver’s cloud record. DriveAlert uses three Warning Stages with 60-second evaluation periods. A new distinct confirmed event immediately advances Stage 1 to Stage 2 or Stage 2 to Stage 3 and begins a new evaluation period. When no new distinct event occurs, persistent qualifying behavior at the evaluation boundary may cause the same progression. The absence of qualifying activity permits incremental de-escalation and eventual return to the normal state. Stages 1 and 2 use the Driver’s selected warning sound, while Stage 3 adds a prerecorded spoken rest advisory and Trusted Contact synchronization eligibility. Each progression from Stage 2 to Stage 3 creates one transition record, including re-entry into Stage 3 after de-escalation. Each completed Stage 3 period creates at most one persistence update when an additional distinct event occurred during the period or a qualifying behavior remains persistently active at its boundary. Individual warning events remain in the Driver’s local history and do not independently create synchronization records. Sharing requires an approved Trusted Contact relationship and internet connectivity. Only eligible records from periods beginning on or after connection approval may be shared, and access depends on the relationship remaining approved. This feature supports secondary awareness and does not operate as an immediate emergency-notification service. Warning Stages do not represent medical or physiological drowsiness severity.
112: The study does not measure DriveAlert’s effect on accident reduction, injury prevention, driver behavior change, or long-term driving habits. It does not cover commercial deployment, vehicle-manufacturer integration, vehicle-control systems, traffic enforcement, or automotive safety certification. DriveAlert cannot apply the brakes, steer the vehicle, reduce its speed, stop the engine, or determine whether the driver is fit to continue driving.
113: Local monitoring can operate without internet after the required account and device setup. Starting a monitoring session requires the appropriate Driver account, an active calibration, a configured and connected camera, and confirmed camera alignment. Account registration, cloud relationship management, remote record review, and external synchronization require internet connectivity.
114: The Android Driver interface supports local monitoring, alert-history review, and descriptive summaries. Trusted Contact interfaces provide access to eligible shared Stage 3 records rather than the Driver’s complete local alert history or camera stream. Record synchronization and notification delivery are separate processes; successful synchronization does not establish that a Trusted Contact has received or viewed a notification.
115: DriveAlert is not intended to replace adequate rest, responsible driving practices, professional medical evaluation, or certified vehicle-safety systems. The study is limited to evaluating whether drowsiness-sign monitoring, warning activation, drowsiness-alert record management, dashboard functions, and Trusted Contact synchronization can operate together under controlled or simulated testing conditions.
116: Significance of the Study
117: The study brings benefits to various stakeholders involved in or affected by the development and implementation of DriveAlert.
118: Drivers. Drivers are the primary beneficiaries of DriveAlert. They may benefit from real-time monitoring of selected visible signs of drowsiness, predefined audio warning alerts with adjustable volume and an enforced minimum level, and a dashboard that records and summarizes drowsiness-alert events. Through the dashboard, Drivers can review alert counts, detected-sign summaries, the highest Warning Stage associated with their stored warning events, alert trends, and Stage 3 synchronization status. Session summaries separately identify the highest intervention stage reached during an individual monitoring session.
119: Trusted Contacts. Trusted Contacts may benefit from accessing synchronization-eligible Stage 3 transition records and Stage 3 persistence updates when internet connectivity becomes available. These records provide secondary awareness when qualifying warning activity reaches or continues at DriveAlert’s maximum intervention stage. The feature supports synchronized record review and does not function as live Driver monitoring or an emergency-response service.
120: Passengers. Passengers may indirectly benefit from DriveAlert because the system provides a warning when a selected visible sign of drowsiness satisfies its established detection condition. The warning may prompt the driver to reassess their condition and take appropriate action, such as stopping and resting.
121: Researchers. The researchers will gain practical experience in Internet of Things development, mobile application design, computer vision-based drowsiness-sign detection, local wireless communication, alert-system implementation, and system evaluation. The study will also strengthen their technical, analytical, problem-solving, documentation, and research skills.
122: Future Researchers. Future researchers may use this study as a reference for related projects involving driver drowsiness monitoring, computer vision, IoT-based safety systems, mobile processing, offline-capable warning systems, alert-record management, and driver-assistance technologies.

158: Figure 1: Flow of the Study
159: Figure 1 shows the flow of the “DriveAlert” study. The process begins with gathering and collecting data on observable signs of driver drowsiness, such as prolonged eye closure, yawning, and head nodding.
160: The next phase involves determining an algorithm that can monitor live video and detect selected visible signs of driver drowsiness, specifically prolonged eye closure, yawning, and head nodding. This phase also includes applying Driver-specific detection thresholds together with sign-specific temporal-confirmation conditions to determine when a confirmed warning event is created.
161: Following this, a direct and offline wireless connection will be designed between the IoT device and the smartphone of the driver. This connection allows the video to be processed instantly through the mobile application without depending on internet or mobile data.
162: To complete the alerting process, an alarm system will be implemented where the IoT device receives wireless signals from the smartphone and triggers an onboard speaker. The onboard speaker will play predefined audio warning alerts using an adjustable warning volume that cannot be muted or reduced below the enforced minimum level during active monitoring.
163: The study also includes the implementation of a mobile dashboard that allows the driver to review drowsiness alert history and basic drowsiness insights. The dashboard displays today’s drowsiness alerts, weekly alert count, synchronization status, most frequently detected sign, highest Warning Stage reached, alert trend, and synchronization summary. A background synchronization system will also be developed to automatically synchronize eligible Warning Stage 3 transition records and Stage 3 persistence updates with an approved Trusted Contact when internet connectivity becomes available.
164: The study follows the Agile Methodology, progressing through iterative stages of planning, requirement analysis, system design, development, testing, deployment, and maintenance. This flexible approach supports continuous improvement and refinement based on feedback from users and testing environments.
165: Ultimately, the output of this study is “DriveAlert,” an Internet of Things safety system designed to assist drivers through drowsiness-sign detection, warning alerts, drowsiness alert history recording, basic drowsiness insights, and Trusted Contact synchronization.
166: Definition of Terms
167: This section contains the specific meaning of the following terms concerning the context used to clarify the study.
168: Alert Trend. It refers to the basic insight showing whether the number of recorded drowsiness alerts is higher, lower, or unchanged compared with the previous period.
169: Artificial Intelligence. It refers to the system capability used by DriveAlert to assist in analyzing driver video and identifying possible drowsiness-related signs.
170: Computer Vision. It refers to the technology used by DriveAlert to process live video and examine visible movements of the driver’s eyes, mouth, face, and head.
171: Driver. It refers to the primary user of DriveAlert who operates a four-wheeled or larger vehicle and uses the IoT device and mobile application to start monitoring sessions, receive drowsiness warnings, review drowsiness-alert records and summaries, and manage Trusted Contact access.
172: Drowsiness Alert. It refers to a confirmed warning event generated after one or more selected visible signs satisfy DriveAlert’s complete Driver-specific measurement and sign-specific temporal-confirmation conditions
173: False Alert. It refers to a warning generated when ordinary or non-target driver behavior incorrectly satisfies the system’s alert condition.
174: Fleet Manager. It refers to a person responsible for supervising a group of organizational or commercial vehicles and their drivers. In the current DriveAlert design, Fleet Manager is identified only as a potential customer segment and not as a separate system actor.
175: Highest Warning Stage. It refers to the highest Warning Stage within the context being summarized. A monitoring-session summary reports the highest intervention stage reached during that session, while an alert-history summary reports the highest stage associated with the stored warning events included in that summary. Neither value represents the Driver’s medical or physiological level of drowsiness.
176: Internet of Things. It refers to the connected hardware and software components of DriveAlert that allow the IoT device and mobile application to exchange video data, connection information, and warning commands.
177: IoT Device. It refers to the DriveAlert hardware unit containing the infrared-capable camera, wireless communication components, and onboard speaker used during driver monitoring.
178: Local Offline Wi-Fi Connection. It refers to the direct local network between the DriveAlert IoT device and the driver’s Android smartphone that does not require internet access or mobile data.
179: Local Processing. It refers to the processing of the transmitted driver video through the Android smartphone instead of sending the video to an internet-based cloud service for drowsiness-sign detection.
180: Missed Detection. It refers to a situation in which the system fails to identify a selected drowsiness-related sign that is visibly demonstrated by the driver.
181: Monitoring Session. It refers to the period beginning when the driver starts drowsiness monitoring and ending when monitoring is stopped. Drowsiness alerts detected during this period are associated with the same session.
182: Onboard Speaker. It refers to the physical speaker installed in the DriveAlert IoT device that produces the selected audio warning after receiving an alert command from the smartphone.
183: Prolonged Eye Closure. It refers to the driver’s eyes remaining closed long enough to satisfy the established duration condition, distinguishing it from an ordinary brief blink.
184: Temporal Confirmation. It refers to the requirement that an observed behavior continue or repeat over a defined period or across consecutive video frames before the system treats the behavior as meeting its established alert threshold.
185: Threshold. It refers to the predefined duration, repetition, movement, or other condition that a detected drowsiness-related sign must satisfy before DriveAlert generates a warning.
186: Trusted Contact. It refers to a user connected to a Driver through an approved relationship who may review eligible shared Stage 3 transition records and Stage 3 persistence updates. The role does not provide access to the Driver’s complete local alert history or full Driver-facing insights. Access depends on the relationship remaining approved. A Trusted Contact does not control monitoring or function as an emergency-response service.
187: Trusted Contact Synchronization. It refers to the automatic upload of eligible Stage 3 transition records and Stage 3 persistence updates for access by an approved Trusted Contact when internet connectivity becomes available. Sharing is limited to eligible records from periods beginning on or after connection approval and does not release the Driver’s earlier complete alert history. Removing the approved relationship revokes the Trusted Contact’s access.
188: Warning Stage Progression. It refers to the process through which DriveAlert changes its active intervention stage. A new distinct confirmed warning event immediately advances Stage 1 to Stage 2 or Stage 2 to Stage 3 and begins a new 60-second evaluation period. Persistent qualifying behavior may also cause escalation at an evaluation boundary. At Stage 3, recurrent or persistent qualifying activity maintains the maximum stage, while the absence of qualifying activity permits incremental de-escalation. Stage progression does not represent increasing medical or physiological drowsiness severity.
189: Warning Trigger. It refers to the system action that activates or reissues the warning associated with the applicable Warning Stage. A warning may be triggered by a new distinct confirmed warning event or by persistent qualifying behavior evaluated at a warning-stage boundary. Recurrent qualifying events during Stage 3 may also reactivate its warning without creating an additional stage.
190: Warning Stage. It refers to the current state of DriveAlert’s warning intervention. Stage 1 provides the initial selected warning sound. Stage 2 retains the same sound as an intermediate warning state. Stage 3 represents the maximum intervention level, combines the selected sound with a prerecorded spoken rest advisory, and introduces Trusted Contact synchronization eligibility. Each stage uses a 60-second evaluation period, although new distinct confirmed events may cause earlier progression. Warning Stages do not represent medical or physiological levels of drowsiness.

221: CHAPTER II
222: REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE AND STUDIES
223: This chapter presents the related literature and studies gathered through the researchers’ careful review and analysis. It provides the theoretical and technical foundation for the development of DriveAlert: An IoT-Based Driver Drowsiness Monitoring and Alert System Using Computer Vision. The review discusses concepts and studies related to driver fatigue and drowsiness, vigilance, human-centered warning systems, computer vision, Internet of Things, mobile-based detection, offline communication, alert mechanisms, drowsiness alert history recording, and Trusted Contact synchronization. This chapter also establishes how the proposed system relates to existing approaches and how DriveAlert contributes to the development of driver drowsiness monitoring and alert systems.
224: Theoretical Background
225: This study is anchored primarily in Vigilance Theory, which explains how sustained attention and alertness may decline during prolonged monitoring tasks. Mackworth (1948) introduced the concept of vigilance decrement through research showing that a person’s ability to detect important signals may decrease over time. In driving, this theory is relevant because drivers must maintain attention for extended periods, particularly during nighttime travel, long-distance driving, and monotonous road conditions. McWilliams and Ward (2021) explained that low-demand and monotonous driving conditions may place drivers in a state of cognitive underload, contributing to vigilance decrement and reduced responsiveness to safety-critical events. For DriveAlert, Vigilance Theory provides a conceptual basis for understanding why maintaining alertness is important during prolonged or monotonous driving. The system itself is limited to monitoring prolonged eye closure, yawning, and head nodding as selected visible signs associated with drowsiness.
226: The study is further guided by Human Factors Engineering and Human-Centered Design principles. Human factors focus on designing systems according to human capabilities, limitations, behaviors, and possible errors. The Federal Highway Administration (n.d.) explained that human factors address the relationship between people and the systems they use, with consideration for human abilities and limitations. This is relevant to driver monitoring because warnings should be clear, timely, noticeable, and understandable without requiring complex interaction from the driver. Ayas et al. (2024) found that driver-drowsiness mitigation systems commonly use auditory warnings and may employ multistage interventions. These principles support DriveAlert’s use of predefined audio warnings, warning escalation, clear monitoring status, and an interface designed to minimize driver interaction during active monitoring while allowing drowsiness-alert records to be reviewed afterward.
227: Together, Vigilance Theory and human-centered design principles provide the theoretical and design foundation of DriveAlert. Vigilance Theory explains the importance of maintaining sustained attention during prolonged or monotonous driving, while Human Factors Engineering and Human-Centered Design guide how the system should communicate warnings and present information according to human capabilities and limitations. These foundations support DriveAlert’s development as a driver-centered drowsiness monitoring and alert system that combines visible-sign monitoring, warning output, drowsiness-alert record review, basic drowsiness insights, and Trusted Contact synchronization.
228: Related Literature
229: Driver sleepiness, sleep deprivation, and prolonged working hours are associated with increased road-traffic risk. Saleem (2022) identified sleepiness and sleep deprivation as contributors to road traffic accidents, while the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (2023) documented crashes and fatalities involving drowsy drivers. In the Philippine setting, Lu (2022) reported that prolonged working hours among bus and truck drivers may contribute to fatigue and sleepiness, thereby increasing road-accident risk. Segun et al. (2024) further identified human behavior and technology-supported interventions among the factors relevant to road safety. Together, these studies establish driver drowsiness as a road-safety concern and support the development of systems that monitor Driver behavior and provide timely warnings.
230: Driver fatigue and drowsiness are related but distinct concepts. Fatigue broadly refers to mental or physical exhaustion, whereas drowsiness describes a sleep-like reduction in alertness that may precede sleep onset. Although some literature uses fatigue as a broader term when discussing reduced alertness, DriveAlert is specifically limited to selected visible signs associated with drowsiness and does not claim to detect fatigue as a whole. Hassan et al. (2026) identified prolonged or slow eyelid closure, yawning, and head nodding as behavioral manifestations of drowsiness. Albadawi et al. (2022) and Jan et al. (2023) likewise identified eye closure, yawning, and head nodding among observable behaviors used in non-intrusive Driver-drowsiness detection. These findings support the selection of prolonged eye closure, yawning, and head nodding as the three visible signs monitored by DriveAlert.
231: Because these signs are externally observable, computer vision provides a non-invasive means of monitoring them without requiring physiological sensors. Albadawi et al. (2023) extracted Eye Aspect Ratio (EAR), Mouth Aspect Ratio (MAR), and head-pose features from facial landmarks using MediaPipe Face Mesh. Essel et al. (2025) similarly examined eye closure and mouth aperture, while Kim et al. (2023) used infrared video and facial landmarks to evaluate eye closure and head orientation. These studies demonstrate that facial landmarks can support a common visual method for examining eye-, mouth-, and head-related behavior. However, facial measurements may still be affected by lighting, camera placement, face orientation, eyeglasses, facial obstruction, and individual facial characteristics.
232: Eye Aspect Ratio represents the degree of eye openness using the relationship among selected eye landmarks and generally decreases as the upper and lower eyelids move closer together. Mouth Aspect Ratio similarly represents the degree of mouth opening and generally increases as the mouth opens wider (Albadawi et al., 2023). Head-pose estimation uses facial reference points to estimate changes in head orientation, while pitch represents upward or downward head rotation and may therefore support head-nodding analysis (Albadawi et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2023). Since ordinary blinking, speaking, and brief downward glances can temporarily produce similar measurements, a single threshold-crossing frame is insufficient to confirm a selected visible sign.
233: Previous studies provide numerical values that can serve as initial references for DriveAlert, but these values should not automatically be applied to every Driver. Albadawi et al. (2023) selected an EAR threshold of 0.20 after evaluating different candidate values and eye characteristics. Higher values increased the possibility of normally open but smaller eyes being classified as closed, while lower values reduced the number of detected closed-eye frames. The same study selected a MAR threshold of 0.50 after considering differences in mouth characteristics; values that were too high could miss wide-mouth events among subjects with smaller mouths, while values that were too low increased the likelihood of ordinary talking or laughing being classified as mouth opening. These findings support 0.20 for EAR and 0.50 for MAR as literature-supported reference values rather than universal operational thresholds.
234: Individual differences support the use of Driver-specific thresholds because fixed facial measurements may not generalize across users with different facial characteristics and normal head orientations. DriveAlert therefore uses four guided calibration phases, each requiring five seconds of valid measurements. During the neutral phase, the Driver faces forward with the eyes open to establish baseline EAR, MAR, and neutral head pitch. During the eyes-closed phase, closed-eye EAR is recorded as a calibration reference, although the operational EAR threshold remains 75% of the neutral open-eye baseline following Ersoy et al. (2026). During the mouth-open phase, the Driver keeps the mouth as open as possible, and the operational MAR threshold is set at 50% of the average open-mouth MAR following the personalized initialization approach of Quiles-Cucarella et al. (2024), expressed as MARThreshold = OpenMouthMAR × 0.50. During the head-down phase, pitch measurements determine the direction of downward rotation relative to the neutral position. DriveAlert then applies a 15° downward deviation from the calibrated neutral head pitch as its operational head-pitch threshold. Xu et al. (2025) similarly used normal forward orientation as a reference, while Shang et al. (2023) reported drowsy head-pitch changes of approximately 12° to 20°, placing the selected 15° deviation within the reported range. These Driver-specific calibration values are stored and used during monitoring.
235: For prolonged eye closure, DriveAlert will use 2.0 seconds as its initial operational confirmation duration. Dong et al. (2026) analyzed distributions of normal and fatigue-related continuous eye-closure events and reported that separation between them became stable at approximately two seconds. Their system therefore used sustained eye closure exceeding two seconds as a fatigue-related criterion. Albadawi et al. (2023) also reported that ordinary blinking occurs on a substantially shorter timescale. DriveAlert consequently adopts 2.0 seconds only to determine whether an observed eye closure qualifies as prolonged eye closure and does not interpret the duration as medical confirmation that the Driver is drowsy. Prolonged eye closure will therefore be confirmed when the Driver's EAR remains below the Driver-specific EAR threshold continuously for at least 2.0 seconds. If the EAR reaches or rises above the threshold before the required duration is completed, the temporal counter resets and no prolonged-eye-closure event is confirmed.
236: Yawning requires a different duration because mouth opening associated with yawning is naturally a longer behavior. Dong et al. (2026) analyzed sustained mouth-opening duration and reported that separation between ordinary mouth movements and fatigue-related yawning became stable at approximately three seconds. Their system consequently used sustained mouth opening exceeding three seconds as its yawning criterion. Quiles-Cucarella et al. (2024) likewise used a personalized MAR threshold together with a three-second mouth-opening condition. DriveAlert will therefore confirm yawning when the Driver’s MAR remains above the Driver-specific MAR threshold continuously for at least 3.0 seconds. If the MAR reaches or falls below the threshold before the required duration is completed, the temporal counter resets and no yawning event is confirmed.
237: Head nodding requires a different confirmation approach because downward head movement is more ambiguous than sustained eye closure or mouth opening. A Driver may naturally look toward the dashboard, vehicle controls, or another location below the normal forward-facing position without being drowsy. Kholerdi et al. (2016) addressed this ambiguity by evaluating the persistence of downward or away-from-road head position rather than treating every downward movement as an immediate warning condition.
238: Kholerdi et al. (2016) considered a prolonged head position significant when the Driver’s head remained dropped or turned away during at least 80% of the preceding three seconds. The authors explained that even when such a prolonged position was not caused by drowsiness, maintaining the head away from the normal forward-facing position for a substantial portion of the observation period could still warrant warning.
239: DriveAlert will therefore initially treat downward head rotation exceeding a fixed 15° deviation from the Driver’s calibrated neutral head position as a candidate head-nodding event rather than immediately confirming the behavior. Calibration establishes the Driver’s neutral head pitch and the direction of downward rotation; it does not determine a different angular deviation threshold for each Driver. The candidate will be confirmed when downward rotation exceeds this threshold in at least 80% of valid observations within a rolling three-second period. If this persistence condition is not satisfied, no head-nodding event is confirmed. The persistence condition is adapted from Kholerdi et al. (2016), while the fixed 15° deviation is a DriveAlert operational choice informed by the approximately 12° to 20° head-pitch changes reported by Shang et al. (2023).
240: The three selected signs are therefore not given identical confirmation procedures because they present different sources of false detection. Sustained EAR measurements help distinguish prolonged eye closure from ordinary blinking, while sustained MAR measurements help distinguish yawning from shorter mouth movements. Head movement is more ambiguous because normal Driver behavior can also involve downward pitch changes, making temporal persistence appropriate. DriveAlert consequently evaluates each selected sign according to its own characteristics rather than requiring prolonged eye closure, yawning, and head nodding to occur simultaneously.
241: During uninterrupted valid observation, DriveAlert groups additional confirmed signs into the current alert episode while a previously active episode sign remains currently confirmed. These additional signs do not independently create another alert episode or advance the Warning Stage. A continuously confirmed sign does not repeatedly create distinct events merely because its measurement remains beyond its threshold. Its detector must be re-armed before the same sign can produce another confirmation. Tracking loss, stale observations, or assessment interruptions may break episode continuity; the system does not assume that a physical behavior remained continuously observable during an unavailable interval.
242: The adopted detection thresholds and confirmation conditions will be evaluated during controlled testing using the actual DriveAlert IoT camera and Android processing environment. Testing will examine false alerts, missed detections, Driver-specific differences, landmark stability, camera placement, performance under different available-light conditions, and processing performance. The purpose of this testing is to verify or refine literature-supported initial conditions under DriveAlert's intended implementation environment rather than assuming that numerical values obtained from another system will transfer perfectly.
243: Computer vision also supports local and non-invasive system implementation. In the Philippine setting, Macalisang et al. (2021) developed Drive-Awake using machine vision to examine eye closure associated with drowsy driving. Fonseca and Ferreira (2025) reviewed behavioral, physiological, and multimodal Driver-monitoring approaches and emphasized that system performance may vary according to datasets, camera conditions, and implementation environments. These considerations support evaluating DriveAlert under its actual deployment conditions.
244: Local mobile processing can reduce dependence on cloud infrastructure by processing data close to its source. Andriulo et al. (2024) discussed edge processing for connected devices, while Martinez-Alpiste et al. (2022) demonstrated the relevance of optimized real-time computer-vision processing for resource-constrained platforms. Li et al. (2024) and Tan and Cao (2024) further indicated that mobile inference may be affected by hardware capability, processing workload, battery condition, and temperature. These findings support DriveAlert's Android-based local processing while recognizing that smartphone capability may influence detection responsiveness.
245: Fourie and Myburgh (2022) demonstrated communication between ESP32-CAM devices and an Android smartphone through a local wireless network for real-time computer-vision functions. Their implementation transmitted camera frames through local Wi-Fi while the smartphone performed processing, demonstrating the technical feasibility of ESP32-based camera hardware communicating with Android without relying on cloud inference. Espressif Systems documentation likewise establishes ESP32 Wi-Fi capability, supporting DriveAlert's local IoT-to-mobile communication architecture.
246: Low-light monitoring is relevant because drowsiness may occur during nighttime, early-morning travel, or in dim vehicle environments. Facial measurements obtained through computer vision may be affected by lighting, camera placement, facial orientation, and image quality. DriveAlert uses an infrared-capable camera without dedicated infrared illumination. Its infrared capability does not itself provide light or guarantee usable facial measurements under extremely low-light or no-light conditions. DriveAlert therefore evaluates facial brightness and contrast before allowing calibration measurements or drowsiness-sign assessment. When lighting is insufficient, assessment pauses even if a face remains detected, and affected frames do not contribute to calibration’s required valid measurement time The system prompts the Driver to restore adequate visibility and resumes assessment when the applicable lighting-quality, recovery, and observation-freshness requirements are met. These checks are image-quality safeguards rather than measures of Driver drowsiness. Their suitability will be evaluated under different available-light conditions using the final hardware configuration.
247: Drowsiness-stage warning intervention begins only after a selected sign satisfies its applicable measurement threshold and temporal-confirmation condition. Separate operational guidance may be issued when facial visibility or lighting is inadequate or when the movement-advisory condition is satisfied. Such guidance does not itself constitute a confirmed drowsiness-sign event, create an additional alert-history episode, or advance the Warning Stage. Detection of a qualifying visible behavior and selection of the corresponding intervention are therefore treated as separate processes. Ayas et al. (2024), in a scoping review of Driver-state-monitoring intervention studies, found that multistage interventions were commonly used and that auditory warnings were among the most frequently employed warning modalities. The reviewed systems generally progressed from an initial caution toward stronger warnings, requested Driver actions, or additional interventions. These findings support progressive warning intervention rather than requiring every confirmed event to produce an identical response.
248: Schwarz et al. (2015) provide a direct basis for DriveAlert’s warning-stage transition architecture. Their drowsiness-mitigation system used a three-stage audio-visual warning in which the same drowsiness threshold was used to trigger each stage. Detection of drowsiness while no mitigation was active initiated Stage 1. During Stage 1, the detection algorithm could either continue classifying the Driver as drowsy or return to an awake state. If the Driver returned to an awake classification, the mitigation was allowed to abate after a fixed delay. However, if another drowsy episode was detected before the current mitigation abated, or if the drowsy state persisted for 60 seconds, the system escalated to Stage 2. Schwarz et al. applied the same logic during Stage 2, allowing the mitigation to either abate to Stage 1 or escalate to Stage 3. Stage 3 represented the maximum intervention level; further escalation was not permitted, although the Stage 3 warning could be reissued when drowsiness persisted or recurred soon afterward. The system permitted only incremental escalation and abatement between adjacent stages.
249: Gaspar et al. (2017) later experimentally evaluated a related staged drowsiness-countermeasure architecture using 72 participants in a high-fidelity driving simulator. Their Driver-state algorithm issued awake or drowsy assessments every six seconds, and the staged interface consisted of three increasingly urgent warning levels. In the evaluated implementation, each countermeasure state expired after 60 seconds. If the Driver was classified as awake when the state expired, the system returned to the previous stage; if drowsiness continued, the system progressed to the next stage. When already at Stage 3, continued drowsiness caused the Stage 3 warning to be triggered again rather than creating an additional stage. Gaspar et al. found that the multistage condition reduced drowsy lane departures relative to the single-stage condition, particularly during the early-morning drives in which participants were expected to be drowsier. These findings provide experimental support for progressive multistage intervention while Schwarz et al. (2015) provides the more detailed basis for the transition mechanics adopted by DriveAlert.
250: DriveAlert will adapt the three-stage transition principles described by Schwarz et al. (2015) to its discrete computer-vision warning events. Because DriveAlert does not repeatedly classify the Driver simply as awake or drowsy, a new distinct confirmed warning event will represent a newly occurring qualifying episode for warning-stage progression. A distinct confirmed warning event is created only after prolonged eye closure, yawning, or head nodding satisfies its complete Driver-specific measurement and temporal-confirmation condition. A single continuous qualifying behavior does not repeatedly generate new events, and qualifying signs occurring during the same overlapping behavioral episode may remain associated with one alert record. These event-management rules prevent a continuous or overlapping episode from causing artificial stage escalation.
251: The first distinct confirmed warning event occurring while no Warning Stage is active will immediately activate Warning Stage 1 and begin a 60-second Stage 1 evaluation period. If another distinct confirmed warning event occurs before the Stage 1 period ends, DriveAlert will interpret the new event as recurrence of qualifying warning activity and will immediately progress to Warning Stage 2. Progression to Stage 2 begins a new 60-second Stage 2 evaluation period. The system therefore does not require the complete Stage 1 period to expire when a new distinct qualifying episode has already occurred. This adapts Schwarz et al.’s (2015) rule in which another drowsy episode detected before the current mitigation abated could cause progression to the next stage.
252: If no additional distinct confirmed warning event occurs during the Stage 1 evaluation period, the system will evaluate the state of the qualifying behavior when the 60-second boundary is reached. If qualifying warning activity remains continuously active at the boundary, DriveAlert will progress to Warning Stage 2 and begin a new 60-second Stage 2 period. If the previously confirmed behavior has ended, no new distinct warning event has occurred, and no qualifying behavior remains active, the system will return to the normal state with no active Warning Stage. This persistence pathway adapts Schwarz et al.’s use of 60 seconds of persistent drowsiness as an alternative reason for escalation while retaining Gaspar et al.’s use of 60-second countermeasure-state boundaries for reevaluation.
253: Warning Stage 2 will follow the same transition principle. If another distinct confirmed warning event occurs while Stage 2 is active, DriveAlert will immediately progress to Warning Stage 3 and begin a new 60-second Stage 3 evaluation period. The new event must represent a genuinely distinct qualifying episode rather than the continuation of the event that previously caused Stage 2. If no new distinct warning event occurs during Stage 2 but qualifying warning activity remains continuously active when the 60-second Stage 2 period expires, the system will likewise progress to Stage 3. If no additional distinct event occurs and the qualifying behavior has ended by the Stage 2 boundary, the system will de-escalate to Warning Stage 1 and begin a new 60-second Stage 1 evaluation period. This preserves incremental progression and de-escalation between adjacent intervention stages.
254: Warning Stage 3 is the maximum DriveAlert intervention stage. Additional distinct confirmed warning events occurring while Stage 3 is active cannot produce a Stage 4. Instead, a new distinct event may reactivate the Stage 3 Driver-directed warning and will be recorded as recurrent qualifying warning activity during the current Stage 3 period. The 60-second Stage 3 evaluation period will not be converted into an additional intervention level. When the Stage 3 period expires, DriveAlert will evaluate whether recurrent qualifying warning activity occurred during the completed period or whether a qualifying behavior remains persistently active at the boundary. If either condition is present, DriveAlert will remain at Stage 3, begin another 60-second Stage 3 evaluation period, and may reissue the Stage 3 intervention. If neither recurrent nor persistent qualifying warning activity is present, the system will de-escalate to Warning Stage 2. Continued absence of qualifying warning activity subsequently permits incremental de-escalation from Stage 2 to Stage 1 and from Stage 1 to the normal state. This maximum-stage behavior follows the principle described by Schwarz et al. (2015), in which Stage 3 could not escalate further but its warning could be reissued when drowsiness persisted or soon recurred.
255: The 60-second value is not used as a confirmation threshold for prolonged eye closure, yawning, or head nodding. Those visible signs retain their independent Driver-specific measurement and temporal-confirmation conditions. Instead, 60 seconds represents the warning-stage evaluation interval used to determine whether persistent warning activity requires escalation or whether the absence of renewed qualifying activity permits de-escalation. A new distinct confirmed warning event may therefore cause earlier progression from Stage 1 to Stage 2 or from Stage 2 to Stage 3 without waiting for the corresponding 60-second period to expire. Conversely, when no new distinct event occurs, the 60-second boundary provides a defined point for evaluating persistent activity or recovery. Schwarz et al. (2015) directly described progression when another drowsy episode occurred before mitigation abated or when the drowsy state persisted for 60 seconds, while Gaspar et al. (2017) experimentally evaluated three-stage warnings using 60-second countermeasure states.
256: Because Schwarz et al. (2015) and Gaspar et al. (2017) used a Driver-state algorithm that classified the Driver as awake or drowsy rather than DriveAlert’s separate EAR-, MAR-, and head-pose-based events, DriveAlert’s mapping of a new distinct confirmed warning event to a newly occurring qualifying episode is an adaptation rather than a direct replication of their detection algorithm. Similarly, DriveAlert interprets continued satisfaction of a selected sign’s confirmed condition at a stage boundary as evidence of persistent qualifying warning activity rather than claiming that the Driver has been continuously and medically confirmed as drowsy. The suitability of this mapping and the 60-second evaluation interval will therefore be examined during controlled testing using the actual DriveAlert implementation.
257: For example, if a Driver first produces a confirmed prolonged-eye-closure event while no stage is active, DriveAlert immediately activates Stage 1. If the eye closure ends and the Driver later produces a distinct confirmed yawning event while Stage 1 remains active, the new event immediately progresses the system to Stage 2 and begins a new Stage 2 evaluation period. If a subsequent distinct head-nodding event occurs while Stage 2 is active, the system immediately progresses to Stage 3. In contrast, when additional signs become confirmed while an existing alert episode remains active under the episode-continuity rule, their signs are merged into that episode and do not independently advance the Warning Stage. Likewise, during uninterrupted valid observation, keeping the eyes continuously closed after one prolonged-eye-closure confirmation does not repeatedly create new distinct alert episodes. Current-stage warning reminders may still be requested, while continued qualifying activity is separately evaluated at the applicable warning-stage boundary.
258: Warning Stage 1 will activate the Driver’s selected predefined warning sound through the IoT device. This represents the initial Driver-directed intervention following a confirmed warning event. Auditory warnings are widely represented in the Driver-state-monitoring literature reviewed by Ayas et al. (2024), while Chandana and Sangeetha (2023) similarly used a sound warning at the first level of their three-level system.
259: Warning Stage 2 will retain the Driver’s selected predefined warning sound without adding a spoken advisory. It serves as an intermediate warning state when qualifying warning activity recurs during Stage 1 or remains persistently active at the Stage 1 evaluation boundary. Although Stages 1 and 2 use the same audio output, they represent different positions within the warning-stage transition process. Ayas et al. (2024) support the broader use of staged interventions, while Chandana and Sangeetha (2023) provide precedent for introducing human-voice warnings after initial sound alerts. DriveAlert adapts these principles by reserving the spoken rest advisory for Warning Stage 3, where it is intended to distinguish the maximum intervention stage from the preceding sound-only warnings. The placement of speech at Stage 3 is a DriveAlert design decision rather than an arrangement directly prescribed by these sources.
260: Chandana and Sangeetha (2023) provide a more direct precedent for reserving external communication for a final intervention level. Their three-level system initially provided a sound warning, progressed to a human-voice warning following repeated warning activity, and used its third level as an alert-notification stage in which information including GPS data was transmitted to the vehicle owner or another concerned person. Their implementation therefore demonstrates an intervention structure progressing from an initial Driver-directed warning, to a stronger Driver-directed warning, and finally to external awareness.
261: Multi-feature Driver-monitoring approaches can reduce dependence on a single facial indicator when visual information is partially obstructed. Jia et al. (2021, 2022) demonstrated that monitoring could continue using the remaining observable facial features when the eyes or mouth were affected by sunglasses or face masks. DriveAlert applies this principle to supported mask and sunglasses detection and to facial measurements reported as unavailable by its processing pipeline. It does not claim to recognize every possible obstruction, such as all hands, phones, hair, scarves, or other objects. When one of the three monitored signs becomes unavailable while two remain reliable, DriveAlert continues in a degraded monitoring state and informs the Driver which capability is unavailable. If any two signs become unavailable, DriveAlert pauses drowsiness-sign monitoring rather than relying on a single behavioral indicator and instructs the Driver to restore sufficient facial visibility before monitoring resumes.
262: Awasekar et al. (2018) provide additional precedent for placing external communication at a highest warning stage. Their Driver Fatigue Detection and Alert System used three fatigue stages based on its own eye-closure and yawning conditions. At Stage 3, the system combined its highest local intervention with communication to predefined contacts through an Android smartphone. DriveAlert does not adopt the fatigue-stage classifications used by Awasekar et al. because DriveAlert’s Warning Stages represent levels of system intervention rather than physiological or medical levels of Driver drowsiness. Their work nevertheless provides additional precedent for associating external communication with a final or highest intervention stage.
263: Fitzharris et al. (2017) further demonstrated the value of supplementing Driver-directed warnings with external awareness in an operational Driver-monitoring environment. Their study examined a camera-based Driver-monitoring system that generated in-cab warnings and was subsequently combined with external monitoring and management feedback when fatigue events were identified. The external feedback condition produced a larger reduction in fatigue-event occurrence than the in-cab-warning condition alone under the study conditions. Although DriveAlert’s Trusted Contact function differs from fleet-management intervention, the study supports the broader principle that external awareness can supplement immediate Driver-directed warnings when monitored warning activity persists.
264: Warning Stage 3 will combine the Driver’s selected predefined warning sound with a fixed prerecorded spoken advisory recommending that the Driver stop in a safe place and take a rest. It will also introduce Trusted Contact synchronization eligibility. When DriveAlert progresses from Stage 2 to Stage 3, one Stage 3 transition record becomes eligible for synchronization with the Driver’s approved Trusted Contact, subject to internet availability. The introduction of speech following earlier sound warnings adapts the progression described by Chandana and Sangeetha (2023), although DriveAlert places speech at Stage 3 rather than at their second intervention level. The exact advisory wording and its placement at Stage 3 are DriveAlert design decisions. Stage 3 represents the maximum system intervention level following recurrent or persistent qualifying warning activity; it does not establish a medically severe level of Driver drowsiness.
265: The three Warning Stages must therefore be interpreted as levels of system intervention rather than measurements of drowsiness severity. Warning Stage 1 represents the initial Driver-directed intervention following the first distinct confirmed warning event. Warning Stage 2 represents an intermediate warning state that retains the selected warning sound when another distinct qualifying episode occurs while Stage 1 remains active or when qualifying warning activity persists through the Stage 1 evaluation boundary. Warning Stage 3 represents the maximum intervention level when qualifying activity again recurs during Stage 2 or persists through the Stage 2 evaluation boundary. DriveAlert does not infer that a Driver at Stage 3 has reached a medically established severity of drowsiness or is physiologically drowsier simply because the highest Warning Stage has been reached.
266: Continued qualifying warning activity while Stage 3 remains active also requires an external-awareness rule that does not generate a separate Trusted Contact synchronization record for every confirmed event. Schwarz et al. (2015) retained Stage 3 as the maximum mitigation stage and allowed the Stage 3 warning to be reissued when drowsiness persisted or recurred. Gaspar et al. (2017) similarly retained Stage 3 as the maximum countermeasure state and retriggered the Stage 3 intervention when continued drowsiness required another escalation. DriveAlert adapts this repeated maximum-stage principle to its Trusted Contact synchronization management.
267: When DriveAlert initially enters Stage 3, one Stage 3 transition record becomes eligible for Trusted Contact synchronization. Individual distinct confirmed warning events occurring during the subsequent 60-second Stage 3 evaluation period continue to be stored in the Driver’s alert history and may reactivate the Stage 3 Driver-directed warning, but they do not each independently create a Trusted Contact synchronization record. Instead, they indicate that recurrent qualifying warning activity occurred during the active Stage 3 period.
268: When the 60-second Stage 3 period expires, DriveAlert evaluates both recurrent and persistent qualifying activity. If at least one additional distinct confirmed warning event occurred during the completed Stage 3 period, or if a qualifying behavior remains persistently active at the Stage 3 boundary, DriveAlert remains at Stage 3, begins another 60-second Stage 3 evaluation period, and creates one Stage 3 persistence update that becomes eligible for Trusted Contact synchronization. If neither condition is present, DriveAlert de-escalates to Stage 2 and no Stage 3 persistence update is created for that completed period.
269: If qualifying warning activity continues across several consecutive Stage 3 periods, at most one persistence update becomes eligible for synchronization for each completed Stage 3 period in which recurrent or persistent qualifying warning activity is established. Individual warning events remain available in the Driver’s detailed alert history but do not independently produce external synchronization records. This separates detailed event recording from the frequency of Trusted Contact updates.
270: The exact rule of generating one Trusted Contact persistence update per completed qualifying Stage 3 period is not a notification frequency directly prescribed by Schwarz et al. (2015), Gaspar et al. (2017), Chandana and Sangeetha (2023), Fitzharris et al. (2017), or the other reviewed studies. Schwarz et al. directly support maintaining Stage 3 as the maximum intervention level and reissuing its warning when drowsiness persists or recurs, while Gaspar et al. directly support repeated Stage 3 intervention following continued drowsiness at a 60-second countermeasure boundary. Chandana and Sangeetha provide precedent for external communication at the final intervention level, and Fitzharris et al. support supplementing Driver-directed warnings with external monitoring or feedback. DriveAlert combines these principles by aligning Trusted Contact persistence updates with its Stage 3 evaluation boundaries. The resulting persistence-update rule is therefore a literature-informed DriveAlert information-management adaptation and will be evaluated during controlled testing for usefulness, unnecessary repetition, and synchronization behavior.
271: For example, several distinct confirmed warning events occurring during one Stage 3 evaluation period do not generate an equal number of Trusted Contact synchronization records. The events remain individually available in the Driver’s alert history, subject to the existing overlap and deduplication rules, while the completed Stage 3 period may produce one persistence update indicating that qualifying warning activity continued. This separates detailed event storage from external-awareness frequency.
272: Trusted Contact synchronization remains subject to Driver approval and internet availability. Only eligible records from periods beginning on or after approval of the Driver–Trusted Contact relationship may be shared. Approval does not release the Driver’s earlier complete alert history, and continued access depends on the relationship remaining approved. If internet connectivity is unavailable when a Stage 3 transition or persistence update becomes synchronization-eligible, the corresponding record is retained in the synchronization queue until connectivity becomes available. Kashyap et al. (2025) demonstrated the use of Firebase and a web dashboard for remote review of Driver-status information, while Kumar et al. (2022) used SMS or email to provide drowsiness-related information to authorized persons or family members. These studies support the broader technical feasibility of making selected Driver information available to an authorized external person. DriveAlert limits this feature to secondary awareness and record review and does not operate as an emergency-response service, guarantee immediate assistance, determine whether the Driver is fit to continue driving, or permit the Trusted Contact to control the Driver’s monitoring session.
273: The Driver’s drowsiness-alert history and basic Drowsiness Insights feature are supported by literature on recorded-event visualization and dashboard-based review. Murphy et al. (2021) and Moens et al. (2021) demonstrated that dashboards can support review of recorded safety-related events and trends. DriveAlert therefore allows Drivers to review confirmed warning events and basic descriptive summaries of stored records. These records and summaries are not intended as medical analysis, accident prediction, confirmation of drowsiness, or determination of the Driver’s fitness to continue driving.
274: Overall, the reviewed literature supports the principal components of DriveAlert while showing that its three selected visible signs require different confirmation procedures. EAR, MAR, and head-pose measurements provide a basis for examining prolonged eye closure, yawning, and head nodding, while Driver calibration addresses individual differences in facial measurements and neutral head orientation. Temporal evidence supports sustained confirmation of prolonged eye closure and yawning, while temporal persistence provides a stronger basis for interpreting ambiguous downward head movement. Driver-monitoring literature further supports progressive multistage warning intervention. Schwarz et al. (2015) provide the primary basis for DriveAlert’s three-stage transition mechanics by describing incremental escalation when another drowsy episode occurs before the current mitigation abates or when drowsiness persists for 60 seconds, together with incremental abatement and Stage 3 as the maximum intervention level. Gaspar et al. (2017) later experimentally evaluated a related three-stage architecture using 60-second countermeasure states and found that staged warnings reduced drowsy lane departures relative to single-stage warnings under the study conditions. Chandana and Sangeetha (2023) provide precedent for introducing human-voice warnings after initial sound alerts and external communication at a final intervention level. DriveAlert adapts these principles by retaining the selected warning sound during Stages 1 and 2 and introducing both a spoken rest advisory and Trusted Contact synchronization eligibility at Stage 3. Fitzharris et al. (2017) provide evidence that external awareness or feedback can supplement immediate Driver-directed warnings. DriveAlert adapts these principles to distinct confirmed computer-vision warning events and controlled Trusted Contact synchronization while treating its Warning Stages as levels of system intervention rather than medical levels of drowsiness. Mobile-processing and IoT literature further supports DriveAlert’s local Android processing and local wireless communication, while its performance under different lighting conditions will be evaluated using the final camera configuration.

276: Related Studies
277: Deployed driver-drowsiness, fatigue, and alertness systems use mobile computer vision, wearable sensors, driver-condition assessment, and physical warning devices to help users recognize reduced alertness. Some systems monitor visible indicators such as eye closure and head movement, while others estimate fatigue using personal information, trip conditions, and reaction-time testing. These systems demonstrate that mobile, wearable, and embedded technologies can provide immediate warnings, although they differ in detection methods, hardware requirements, offline capability, record management, and user support.
278: Drowsy Driving Alert is a deployed mobile application that uses artificial intelligence and the smartphone’s front camera to monitor the driver’s face and eyes. The application distinguishes normal blinking from extended eye closure and provides audio and visual alerts when drowsiness is detected. It can operate alongside Google Maps and Apple Maps, while facial processing is performed locally on the smartphone. However, the phone must be positioned where the camera can clearly view the driver. Lighting, camera obstruction, device performance, and background-operation restrictions may also delay or interrupt warnings.
279: WingDriver is a smartphone-based driver-monitoring system that uses computer vision and artificial intelligence to detect drowsiness, fatigue, distraction, and inattention. It provides immediate audible or visual warnings to help the driver regain attention. The provider also offers related business solutions for fleets, ride-sharing services, insurers, and vehicle manufacturers. However, monitoring depends on the smartphone camera maintaining a usable view of the driver and on the mobile device having sufficient processing capability. Broader business deployment also requires coordination with the provider.
280: Fatigue Alert – Stay Awake is a deployed application that uses supported AirPods or Beats headphones to monitor the user’s head posture. When downward head movement indicates possible drowsiness, the application provides warnings through sound, vibration, system notifications, or Apple Watch haptic feedback. It can continue operating in the background or while the device screen is locked. However, the system requires compatible Apple devices and supported headphones and focuses mainly on head-posture monitoring rather than facial indicators. It is also presented as a general stay-awake application rather than a system exclusively intended for driving.
281: Anti Sleep Pilot is a dashboard-mounted device and mobile application that continuously calculates a driver’s fatigue level. Its calculation considers the driver’s personal risk profile, fatigue condition before the trip, and driving information registered through built-in sensors. It also administers periodic reaction-time tests and provides audible and visual recommendations when the driver should take a break. However, the system requires initial profile configuration and periodic interaction from the driver. Its documentation also notes that low-speed traffic, GPS conditions, and some driving situations may affect trip-state interpretation or cause distraction.
282: The alertme Drowsy Driver Device is a commercially deployed wearable warning device developed by resqme, Inc. It is worn behind the ear and detects downward head nodding associated with possible dozing. When the user’s head tilts forward by approximately 15 to 20 degrees, the battery-powered device produces a 90-decibel audible alarm. The device does not require a smartphone, mobile application, or internet connection. However, it must be worn correctly, detects only head-nodding movement, and provides only a direct audible warning.
283: The reviewed systems demonstrate different approaches to driver-state monitoring and warning. Drowsy Driving Alert and WingDriver use smartphone cameras and artificial intelligence to monitor visible driver behaviors. Fatigue Alert – Stay Awake uses wearable motion information to monitor head posture. Anti Sleep Pilot estimates fatigue through driver profiles, sensor information, and reaction-time testing. Meanwhile, alertme uses a simple wearable mechanism to detect downward head movement and produce an immediate alarm.
284: The systems collectively show the progress and remaining integration gaps in driver-drowsiness and alertness monitoring. Smartphone-based applications provide accessible camera monitoring but depend on phone placement, facial visibility, and device performance. Wearable applications require compatible accessories, while fatigue-estimation systems require profile configuration and periodic driver interaction. Simple physical alarms can operate without internet access but generally monitor only one behavior and do not provide organized records.
285: Overall, DriveAlert combines selected driver-monitoring and record-management functions in an external-camera and Android-based prototype. Its configuration includes an external IoT camera with an infrared-capable sensor, Android-based local processing, offline IoT-to-mobile communication, monitoring of prolonged eye closure, yawning, and head nodding, and onboard speaker warning output. Confirmed warning events are stored locally for Driver review and descriptive summaries, while separate eligible Stage 3 transition records and persistence updates support approved Trusted Contact sharing. DriveAlert investigates whether these functions can operate together under controlled testing conditions.

The review will determine whether the system requirements were achieved and whether revisions are needed. Identified improvements will be added to the project backlog and addressed in the succeeding sprint. The final review will verify that DriveAlert satisfies the study objectives, scope, system requirements, and evaluation criteria.

602: Figure 6.2: Use Case Diagram cont’d
603: Figure 6 presents the use case diagram of the “DriveAlert” system. The diagram shows two main actors: the Driver and the Trusted Contact. Both actors can register, log in, view their profile, update their profile, and log out of the application. The Driver can view setup requirements, allow the required app permissions, enable the mobile hotspot, connect the IoT device, view the device connection status, and reconnect the device when needed. The Driver can also start a monitoring session, preview camera alignment, run drowsiness-sign detection, detect selected visible signs associated with drowsiness, view monitoring status, and stop the monitoring session. For alert-related functions, the Driver can receive drowsiness alerts, receive onboard speaker warning output, view alert status, view drowsiness alert history, view alert details, and view basic drowsiness insights. The Driver can also add Trusted Contacts and send Trusted Contact requests. The Trusted Contact can view pending requests, accept or decline requests, view connected drivers, view shared drowsiness-alert records, view shared alert history, view shared alert details, and view shared basic drowsiness insights. These use cases show that the Driver controls the device setup, monitoring process, drowsiness-sign detection, alert viewing, drowsiness alert summary review, and Trusted Contact request process. On the other hand, the Trusted Contact is limited to accepting or declining requests and reviewing shared drowsiness-alert records and related basic drowsiness insights from connected drivers. Unlike the Driver, the Trusted Contact does not control monitoring, device connection, drowsiness-sign detection, or warning output.

823: Database Design
824: This section refers to the process of creating a detailed data structure for storing, managing, and organizing data in a database management system (DBMS). It involves designing the layout, relationships, and rules that govern how data is stored and accessed within the database.
825: 
826: Figure 58: Entity Relationship Diagram
827: FK denotes logical references across Room and Firestore. Repeated account references remain in the attributes; only declared Room foreign keys are enforced by SQL.
828: 
829: Figure 53 presents the Entity Relationship Diagram (ERD) of DriveAlert, showing the relationships among entities for user accounts, connection codes, Driver calibration, provisioned IoT devices, monitoring sessions, confirmed warning events, Trusted Contact connections, shared Stage 3 records, preferences, and monitoring logs. The diagram combines Room’s local data structures with Cloud Firestore’s cloud records. Its FK labels represent logical references; relationships explicitly declared as Room foreign keys are enforced within the local database.
830: The User entity stores profile information, including the user’s name, email address, phone number, role, account status, registration date, and connection code. The role identifies whether the user operates as a Driver, Trusted Contact, or both. ConnectionCode associates a unique connection code with its owner and stores the account’s display name, code creation date, and active status. These codes allow users to find another account when requesting a connection.
831: DriverPreference stores the selected warning sound, preferred volume, notification preferences, history-retention setting, and update time. MonitoringLogPreference separately stores the Driver’s retention setting for completed monitoring logs.
832: DriverCalibration stores personalized EAR, MAR, and head-pitch thresholds, calibration measurements, detector schema version, calibration date, and active status. A Driver may have several calibration records over time. Each MonitoringSession references the calibration record used during that session, preserving the association between the monitoring activity and its detection thresholds.
833: ProvisionedDevice stores the Driver’s saved camera connection, including the device identifier, hostname, last known IP address, stream port, stream path, last connection time, and active status. Its composite primary key consists of driverUserId and deviceId. MonitoringSession identifies the Driver, camera device, calibration record, session start and end times, session status, and highest recorded Warning Stage.
834: TrustedContactConnection manages relationships between Drivers and Trusted Contacts. It records the participants, requesting user, target connection code, connection status, and request, approval, decline, and revocation times. A Driver may connect with multiple Trusted Contacts, while a Trusted Contact may connect with multiple Drivers. Approval is required before future eligible Stage 3 records can be shared.
835: Alert stores confirmed warning events recorded during a monitoring session. Its attributes include the detection time, Warning Stage at detection, onboard alarm status, and alarm-trigger time. AlertSign associates each alert with its recorded visible signs, including prolonged eye closure, yawning, and head nodding. Warning Stages describe increasing system intervention and do not represent medical drowsiness severity.
836: StageSyncRecord stores local Stage 3 synchronization records, including the source session, record type, warning-period boundaries, event count, sharing eligibility, synchronization status, upload attempts, and cloud document identifier. StageSyncRecordSign associates each synchronization record with its detected signs. Eligible records are published to SharedStage3Record in Cloud Firestore, where approved Trusted Contacts can review the shared warning period, signs, event count, and synchronization information. Shared records provide historical awareness rather than live monitoring.
837: MonitoringLog stores a session’s diagnostic log, including its start and end times, status, and recording-gap indicator. MonitoringLogEvent stores individual timeline entries, such as detected signs, measured values, thresholds, durations, calibration references, Warning Stages, and alarm-response results. Overall, the ERD supports personalized monitoring, confirmed warning history, approved Trusted Contact sharing, account connections, configurable preferences, and review of recorded monitoring activity.

1055: Figure 41: DriveAlert IoT Hardware Architecture
1056: Figure 41 presents the DriveAlert IoT Hardware Architecture and illustrates how the IoT device, warning components, power source, and Android smartphone work together during drowsiness monitoring. A regulated 5-volt rechargeable power source supplies power to the ESP32-S3 and the components connected through the solderless breadboard. The breadboard serves as the hardware interconnection platform for distributing power and connecting the DFPlayer Mini, MOSFET driver, red status LED, infrared LEDs, and other supporting components.
1057: The Freenove ESP32-S3 CAM functions as the main controller of the IoT device. It operates the OV2640 NoIR camera, which captures the driver’s face and head movements. Four 850 nm infrared LEDs provide additional illumination in low-light conditions, while the MOSFET driver allows the ESP32-S3 to control the infrared LEDs safely without drawing their operating current directly from a GPIO pin. This supports the system’s monitoring of prolonged eye closure, yawning, and head nodding under dim vehicle conditions.
1058: The ESP32-S3 transmits the camera video to the Android smartphone through a local Wi-Fi connection. The smartphone processes the video, evaluates the detected drowsiness signs according to the established thresholds, and sends warning commands back to the IoT device. It also sends connection keep-alive messages to confirm that the application remains connected. When the ESP32-S3 stops receiving these messages, the red status LED blinks to indicate that the smartphone application is disconnected.
1059: When a drowsiness threshold is reached, the smartphone sends a warning command to the ESP32-S3. The controller then instructs the DFPlayer Mini to play a predefined warning sound stored on the microSD card. The sound is produced through the 4-ohm, 3-watt onboard speaker. The warning volume may be adjusted, but it cannot be muted or reduced below the enforced minimum level. The monitoring and warning process operates through local Wi-Fi without requiring internet access, while internet connectivity is only needed for later synchronization of alert records with an approved Trusted Contact.
